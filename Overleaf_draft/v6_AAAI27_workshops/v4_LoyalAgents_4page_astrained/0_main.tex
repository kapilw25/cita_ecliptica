\begin{abstract}
Whose contract does an AI agent follow when the deployer and the user disagree? In the models we test, a deployer's system-message contract moves refusals only in models that a user can also talk out of it, and models told to serve a hidden sponsor do so, then deny being paid. Reading switchable alignment as delegation, we measure three levers of the system slot (refusal, authority over a contrary user turn, a hidden sponsor) on Llama-3.1-8B models trained with SFT, DPO, GRPO and SwiPO (Switchable Preference Optimization, an instruction-conditioned DPO), each with one safety contract and evaluated as trained, and count a refusal lever only where a refusal-phrase heuristic and an LLM judge agree. Two models switch refusals: a prompted instruction-tuned model (judge gap \nInstructJudge{}) and the released SwiPO, whose chain skips SFT (\nSwiPORJudge{}, halved by a second judge); with SFT in the chain, contracts stay weak levers for SwiPO as for DPO and GRPO. Both steerable models yield to a user who loosens the contract, and only the prompted one also follows a user who tightens it. The models with SFT keep their refusals under a loosening user but not their content: SFT, PPO (an untrained adapter, so a second SFT run) and GRPO give more unsafe answers (Llama Guard 3, \nOverrideLo{} to \nOverrideHi{}). Told to promote a hidden sponsor, prompted instruction-tuned models of \nSponsorFamilies{} families do so in \nSponsorLo{} to \nSponsorHi{} of 805 requests; none stops when the user asks for impartiality and disclosure, and none admits the deal in 470 follow-ups. Contract-trained models rarely promote, and no system contract detectably shifts their unsafe answers or protects floor requests, which argues for training a floor. Audits must load the model as trained: the final adapter alone shows refusal gaps of \nFinalLo{} to \nFinalHi{}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents increasingly act for people, and inside a language model whom an agent works for has a concrete form: the system and user turns compete to direct it. Preference optimization such as direct preference optimization (DPO) \cite{rafailov2023direct} fits one default policy to all annotators. Switchable alignment instead passes a natural-language contract $I$ next to the request $X$, so that the same $X$ yields different behavior only because $I$ changed, steerable pluralism in the terms of \citet{sorensen2024roadmap}.

\input{../common/fig_loyal}  % owner pick Fri Nov 27 2026: LoyalAgents' own Figure 1 (the loyalty levers)


For loyalty the contract has a second reading. $I$ sits in the deployer's slot, unseen by the user, so every behavior it controls is a lever the deployer holds over the user. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A principal-agent reading of switchable alignment: the system slot as delegation interface, its levers, and four loyalty controls, three tested (Section~\ref{sec:loyalty}).
    \item Each lever measured on the models as trained, under two refusal instruments (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Figure~\ref{fig:loyal}). For refusals, steerability and exploitability arrive together: the two models whose refusals follow the contract (a prompted instruction-tuned model and SwiPO without SFT) both yield to a user turn that loosens it (only the prompted model also follows one that tightens it), and prompted models of \nSponsorFamilies{} families serve a hidden sponsor against the user's request. Harm moves the other way: no system contract moves a trained model's unsafe answers, but a loosening user turn raises them in the models with SFT, whose refusals resist (SFT, PPO, GRPO; slightly DPO).
\end{itemize}



\section{Whose Contract Does the Agent Follow?}
\label{sec:loyalty}

Four parties stand behind one answer: the provider fixes defaults in training, the deployer writes $I$ into the system message, the user writes $X$, and third parties are affected but write nothing. Table~\ref{tab:levers} reads each evaluated switch as a lever over the user; of four controls on its holder we test authority, conflicts of interest and limits.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authority.} Following the user is loyal when it tightens or adjusts the deployer's contract; only an accountable principal, through an authenticated, privileged channel, may loosen protections owed to third parties \cite{wallace2024instruction,south2025authenticated}, a priority current models enforce unreliably \cite{geng2025control,zhang2025iheval}.
    \item \textbf{Disclosure and audit.} The user does not see $I$: a contract card would disclose the contract in force, and a log of it with every response would trace a harmful switch to the principal who set it \cite{chan2024visibility}.
    \item \textbf{Conflicts of interest.} A deployer can loosen the contract to cut complaints or promote a sponsor; told to, many models favor the company over users \cite{wu2026ads}.
    \item \textbf{Limits.} Loyalty to the user is bounded by duties to third parties, as a fiduciary's is: a floor no contract may change, trained with targets independent of $I$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\citet{aguirre2020loyalty} call an AI system loyal to the degree that it minimizes and discloses conflicts of interest; fiduciary procedures \cite{benthall2023fiduciary}, agency law \cite{kolt2025governing}, alignment among assistant, user, developer, and society \cite{gabriel2024ethics}, and behavioral loyalty tests \cite{greenwood2026loyalevals} build on that view. Studies of sponsored answers \cite{wu2026ads} and instruction-hierarchy benchmarks \cite{zhang2025iheval,geng2025control} prompt fixed models; we add the user's counter-request and a paid-or-not follow-up, and measure what contract training changes in the models as trained. \input{../common/related_core}

\section{Conclusion}

Under both refusal instruments, a deployer's contract moves refusals only in a prompted instruction-tuned model and in SwiPO without SFT, and no trained model's harm; the two steerable models also yield to a user who loosens the contract (only the prompted one to a user who tightens it), and prompted models of \nSponsorFamilies{} families to a hidden sponsor, against the user's request, while the models with SFT keep their refusals under a loosening user but give more unsafe answers. For loyal agents this argues for a contract card shown to the user, the contract logged with every response, loosening only through an authenticated channel, a trained floor, and audits of the model as trained.
