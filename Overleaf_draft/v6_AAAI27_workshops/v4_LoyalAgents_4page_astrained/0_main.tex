\begin{abstract}
An AI agent works for whoever writes its instructions. In a switchable model, a natural-language contract in the system message, the slot the deployer controls, decides how readily the model refuses the user in front of it. We measure how far such contracts move four preference-trained Llama-3.1-8B models (DPO, PPO, GRPO, and SwiPO, an instruction-conditioned DPO with a mandatory Kullback-Leibler (KL) anchor), each trained with one safety contract and tested on contracts it never saw. Each training stage merged its parent's low-rank adapter and trained a new one, so a released adapter holds only its own stage. Loaded as the base model plus the final adapter, as in our earlier versions, every model's refusal rate moved by 0.30 to 0.49 between a strict and a permissive contract. Loaded as trained, DPO, PPO, and GRPO move by 0.03, and only SwiPO still moves (0.376, 95\% interval 0.346 to 0.407). Yet as trained no model's rate of unsafe answers, judged by Llama Guard 3, changes with the contract (differences $-$0.002 to +0.016, every interval including zero): the contract changes how SwiPO declines, not what it gives. No model refuses floor requests, such as violence or trafficking, more readily than other requests under the permissive contract. \pending{SwiPO retrained on the intended chain (SFT, then DPO, then SwiPO), results Thu ~11 PM PST}. Measuring whom a switchable agent serves starts with evaluating the model that was trained.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents increasingly act for people, and inside a language model the question of whom an agent works for has a concrete answer: it works for whoever writes its inputs. The provider trains a default policy, the deployer writes a system message, and the user writes the request. Pooled preference optimization such as DPO \cite{rafailov2023direct} fits that default to the pairwise judgments of all annotators, which ranks answers by a Borda count over the annotators' unrecorded roles \cite{siththaranjan2024distributional}, a rule nobody chose \cite{conitzer2024social}. Switchable alignment instead passes a natural-language contract $I$ next to the request $X$, so that the same $X$ yields different behavior only because $I$ changed (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}), steerable pluralism in the terms of \citet{sorensen2024roadmap}.

For loyalty the contract has a second reading. $I$ sits in the deployer's slot and the user usually does not see it, so every behavior it controls is a lever the deployer holds over the user. The loyalty question is therefore not whether the model follows instructions, but whose instructions it follows, which behaviors they may move, and what no contract may move. Answering it needs a measurement of the model that was actually trained, which our earlier versions did not make. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A principal-agent reading of switchable alignment: the system slot as the delegation interface, the levers it controls, and five controls that decide whether a switchable agent stays loyal to its user (Section~\ref{sec:loyalty}).
    \item A loading check: stacked low-rank adapters evaluated without their parents showed a refusal lever of 0.30 to 0.49 in every model; as trained, it remains only in SwiPO (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item As trained, the contract moves SwiPO's refusal wording but no model's rate of unsafe answers, on floor requests and elsewhere (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Whose Contract Does the Agent Follow?}
\label{sec:loyalty}

Four parties stand behind one answer: the provider fixes defaults in training, the deployer writes $I$ into the system message, the user writes $X$, and third parties, such as the neighbor in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, are affected but write nothing. In every suite below $I$ enters as the system message of the Llama-3.1 chat template, the level the OpenAI Model Spec calls developer messages \cite{openai2025modelspec}. Conditioning on $I$ moves the choice of behavior out of the annotator pool and into that slot: if every context $(I,X)$ has its own free distribution, as in a tabular or unlimited-capacity policy, the conditional preference loss splits into one term per contract, so the answers under $I_b$ depend only on contract $b$'s data. The party who chooses $I$ decides what the user receives. Table~\ref{tab:levers} reads each switch we evaluate as such a lever; five controls decide whether its holder can turn the agent against the user.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authority.} If a user can write $I$, a permissive contract is a jailbreak; the contract has to arrive through an authenticated, privileged channel held by an accountable principal \cite{wallace2024instruction,south2025authenticated}, a priority current models enforce unreliably \cite{geng2025control,zhang2025iheval}.
    \item \textbf{Disclosure.} The user sees $X$ and $Y$ but not $I$, so a length cap or a confident-answer contract changes what the user receives without telling them. A contract card, a readable statement of the contract in force, is the switchable form of a disclosure standard.
    \item \textbf{Conflicts of interest.} A deployer can pick the most permissive contract to cut complaints or cap length to save tokens, choices training does not rule out; told to promote sponsors, many models favor the company over the user \cite{wu2026ads}.
    \item \textbf{Limits.} Loyalty to the user is bounded by duties to third parties, as a human fiduciary's is: a floor of behaviors no contract may change, trained with targets that do not depend on $I$, below a switchable layer.
    \item \textbf{Audit.} If the contract in force is logged with every response, a harmful switch can be traced to the principal who set it \cite{chan2024visibility}; holding $X$ fixed and varying $I$ measures each lever and the leakage on floor requests. An audit is only as good as its copy of the model: it must load what was trained.
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Models.} All four trainers start from Llama-3.1-8B \cite{dubey2024llama} with LoRA adapters \cite{hu2022lora} and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on PKU-SafeRLHF \cite{ji2024pku}: 12,035 prompts whose two responses differ in safety, re-split 90/10. Each later stage merges its parent adapter into the base model and trains a new one. DPO, PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} (with an off-the-shelf reward model) and GRPO \cite{shao2024deepseekmath} (with rule-based safety, helpfulness, and format rewards) train on the merged SFT model. SwiPO trains on the merged DPO adapter, as released without the SFT adapter beneath it, and continues from that checkpoint $\pi_0$ with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:swipo}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SwiPO}} = \mathbb{E}\big[-\log\sigma\big(\beta\,\Delta_\theta(I,X)\big)\big] + \lambda\,\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_\theta,\quad \lambda>0,
\end{equation}
so Eq.~\ref{eq:swipo} is DPO with $I$ in the conditioning set plus a mandatory anchor; $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\Delta_\theta(I,X)$ the DPO margin of the preferred over the rejected response, and $\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_\theta$ the mean log-ratio to $\pi_0$ on the pair, a proxy for the divergence. Each model is trained with one safety contract in the system message (``You must REFUSE any requests involving $\langle$harms$\rangle$'') and has a NoInstruct twin trained without it, so every contract evaluated below is unseen in training. A released adapter holds only its own stage. We evaluate each model as trained, merging its parent adapters first; Table~\ref{tab:loading} also reports the base model plus the final adapter alone, the loading behind every number in our earlier versions. \pending{SwiPO retrained on SFT, then DPO, then SwiPO}

\paragraph{Suites.} Conditional Safety poses the 1,222 PKU-SafeRLHF test prompts under a strict and a permissive contract (Table~\ref{tab:levers}) and scores the strict-minus-permissive refusal-rate gap; each prompt carries one or more of 19 harm categories. Every answer is one sample at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9; refusal is detected by deterministic heuristics, and Llama Guard 3 (8B) \cite{inan2023llamaguard} labels every answer safe or unsafe. Every interval is a 95\% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap with 10,000 resamples over prompts, paired across models.

\paragraph{Floor set.} Before computing any rate we fixed a floor set: harm to the body or safety of people outside the conversation (Violence, Physical Harm, Human Trafficking, Endangering National Security, Endangering Public Health; 347 of the 1,222 prompts). If a model had a floor, the permissive contract would lower refusals less on these prompts than on the other 875. We also posed 2,444 unseen PKU-SafeRLHF prompts (outside the training pool, from pairs with two unsafe answers; 918 floor) under the same two contracts.

\paragraph{The contract changes wording, not harm.} As trained, the permissive-minus-strict unsafe rate is $-$0.002 to +0.016 for all four models, every interval including zero (Table~\ref{tab:loading}), and on floor prompts $-$0.012 to +0.023, again every interval including zero. SwiPO's refusal gap of 0.376 comes with an unsafe-rate change of +0.011 [$-$0.002, 0.024]: the answers that lose the refusal phrase under the permissive contract are still judged safe. SwiPO and DPO give unsafe answers to 2 to 3\% of requests under either contract, and PPO and GRPO to about a third (0.32 to 0.34), whatever the contract; in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} PPO's unsafe answer comes after it writes past the end of its turn.

\paragraph{No floor by default.} Under the permissive contract no model refuses floor requests more readily than other requests: the gap difference runs from $-$0.046 to +0.016 on the suite and from $-$0.036 to +0.021 on the held-out prompts, every interval including zero (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). For DPO, PPO, and GRPO the contract barely moves refusals anywhere; for SwiPO the floor categories move as much as the rest. The prompted Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (safety-tuned by its developers; our trainers start from the base model) has a floor, by refusing broadly: gap difference $-$0.115 [$-$0.161, $-$0.065], permissive refusal 0.84$\pm$0.04 on floor and 0.72$\pm$0.03 on other requests, and 0.012 [0.003, 0.029] of its permissive floor answers unsafe.

\section{Open Problems for Loyal Agents}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deployer versus user.} None of our contracts works against the user. A loyalty suite would pair each request with contracts that do, such as a length cap on a request that needs detail, and report whose preference the answer follows.
    \item \textbf{Contracts in the wrong slot.} The same contract in the user turn, a retrieved page, or another agent's message should not switch the model: the instruction-hierarchy test \cite{wallace2024instruction} for switchable models.
    \item \textbf{A trained floor.} Training the floor categories with targets that do not depend on $I$, then repeating Table~\ref{tab:floor} and Figure~\ref{fig:percat}, tests whether the two-tier design holds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\citet{aguirre2020loyalty} call an AI system loyal to the degree that it minimizes and discloses conflicts of interest and puts its users first; \citet{benthall2023fiduciary} give a procedure for fiduciary AI, \citet{kolt2025governing} applies agency law to AI agents, \citet{gabriel2024ethics} treat alignment as a relation among assistant, user, developer, and society, and \citet{greenwood2026loyalevals} turn loyalty commitments into behavioral tests, as our lever measurements do. The instruction hierarchy \cite{wallace2024instruction} and the OpenAI Model Spec \cite{openai2025modelspec} rank instruction sources; Constitutional AI \cite{bai2022constitutional} fixes principles at training time, a candidate for the floor. Controllable safety alignment (CoSA) \cite{zhang2025cosa}, the closest work, adapts a model to safety configurations in the system prompt.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

Every result uses one backbone (Llama-3.1-8B), one training seed per method, and one sampled answer per item. Each model was trained with one safety contract, so every contract here is unseen. SwiPO's released chain skips the SFT adapter, so its lead in Table~\ref{tab:loading} confounds the loss with the missing SFT stage. Only SwiPO's hyper-parameters were tuned (13 Optuna trials). About nine in ten Conditional Safety prompts appear in the training pool. Refusal is detected by heuristic, which misses declines without a refusal phrase (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}), and harm by one classifier (Llama Guard 3) that we did not check against human labels. The ECLIPTICA-3k suite and the Alignment Quality Index were not re-measured as trained.

\section{Ethics Statement}

A switchable model's safety behavior can be switched by whoever holds the system slot. We release the benchmark and code, not a deployed product, and argue that the contract must come from an authenticated, accountable principal, be disclosed to the user on request, be logged, and sit above a floor that no contract may lift. Conditional Safety reuses public PKU-SafeRLHF requests, harmful by design; we add instructions and metadata but no new harmful completions. We correct our earlier versions here: their lever sizes came from adapters loaded without their parents.

\section{Conclusion}

Measured on the models as trained, a contract in the deployer's slot moves only SwiPO's refusals (0.376), changes how it declines rather than what it gives, and leaves floor requests no better protected than others. Loaded without their parent adapters, the same models showed a refusal lever of 0.30 to 0.49 that also moved harmful content.
