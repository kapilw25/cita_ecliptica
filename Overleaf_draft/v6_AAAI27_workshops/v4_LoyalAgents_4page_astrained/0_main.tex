\begin{abstract}
We read switchable alignment as delegation: the system message is how a deployer directs an agent acting for a user. We measure three levers (refusal, authority over a contrary user turn, a hidden sponsor) in Llama-3.1-8B models trained with DPO, GRPO, and SwiPO (an instruction-conditioned DPO with a log-ratio penalty to the reference model), each with one safety contract and evaluated as trained, every parent low-rank adapter merged, plus a released PPO model whose untrained adapter replicates the SFT and base models. A lever counts only where a refusal-phrase heuristic and an LLM judge agree. Two models switch refusals: a prompted instruction-tuned model (judge gap \nInstructJudge{}) and the released SwiPO, whose chain skips supervised fine-tuning (SFT) (\nSwiPORJudge{}). Steerability and exploitability arrive together: under both instruments both yield to a user turn that loosens the contract, which the models with SFT resist, and the prompted one, told to promote a hidden sponsor, does so in half of 805 requests even when the user asks for impartiality and disclosure, then denies being paid; contract-trained models rarely promote. With SFT in the chain, refusal and sponsor contracts stay weak levers: DPO and GRPO keep a strict-minus-permissive phrase gap of \nRivalGap{}, and SwiPO after SFT \nSwiPOS{} (95\% interval \nSwiPOSLo{} to \nSwiPOSHi{}), not separable from DPO (\nSwiPOSvsDPO{}, \nSwiPOSvsDPOLo{} to \nSwiPOSvsDPOHi{}). The pretrained model's phrase gap of \nBaseGap{} (\nBaseGapLo{} to \nBaseGapHi{}) is mostly wording the judge does not see (\nBaseJudge{}). No trained model shows a detectable shift in unsafe answers under the deployer's contract (Llama Guard 3) or floor-specific protection, which motivates training a floor. Audits must load the model as trained: the final adapter alone shows \nFinalLo{} to \nFinalHi{}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents increasingly act for people, and inside a language model whom an agent works for has a concrete form: the system and user turns compete to direct it. Preference optimization such as DPO \cite{rafailov2023direct} fits one default policy to all annotators. Switchable alignment instead passes a natural-language contract $I$ next to the request $X$, so that the same $X$ yields different behavior only because $I$ changed, steerable pluralism in the terms of \citet{sorensen2024roadmap}.

\input{../common/fig_loyal}  % owner pick Fri Nov 27 2026: LoyalAgents' own Figure 1 (the loyalty levers)


For loyalty the contract has a second reading. $I$ sits in the deployer's slot, unseen by the user, so every behavior it controls is a lever the deployer holds over the user. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A principal-agent reading of switchable alignment: the system slot as delegation interface, its levers, and five loyalty controls, three tested (Section~\ref{sec:loyalty}).
    \item Each lever measured on the models as trained, under two refusal instruments (Section~\ref{sec:results}): steerability and exploitability arrive together: of the two models whose refusals follow the contract (a prompted instruction-tuned model and SwiPO without SFT), both also yield to a user turn that loosens the contract (both instruments), and the prompted one serves a hidden sponsor against the user's request; no system contract moves a trained model's harm; a contrary user turn raises it after SFT, PPO and GRPO.
\end{itemize}



\section{Whose Contract Does the Agent Follow?}
\label{sec:loyalty}

Four parties stand behind one answer: the provider fixes defaults in training, the deployer writes $I$ into the system message, the user writes $X$, and third parties are affected but write nothing. Table~\ref{tab:levers} reads each evaluated switch as a lever over the user; of five controls on its holder we test authority, conflicts of interest and limits.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authority.} Following the user is loyal when it tightens or adjusts the deployer's contract; only an accountable principal, through an authenticated, privileged channel, may loosen protections owed to third parties \cite{wallace2024instruction,south2025authenticated}, a priority current models enforce unreliably \cite{geng2025control,zhang2025iheval}.
    \item \textbf{Disclosure.} The user does not see $I$; a contract card stating the contract in force would disclose it.
    \item \textbf{Conflicts of interest.} A deployer can loosen the contract to cut complaints or promote a sponsor; told to, many models favor the company over users \cite{wu2026ads}.
    \item \textbf{Limits.} Loyalty to the user is bounded by duties to third parties, as a fiduciary's is: a floor no contract may change, trained with targets independent of $I$.
    \item \textbf{Audit.} If the contract in force is logged with every response, a harmful switch can be traced to the principal who set it \cite{chan2024visibility}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\citet{aguirre2020loyalty} call an AI system loyal to the degree that it minimizes and discloses conflicts of interest; fiduciary procedures \cite{benthall2023fiduciary}, agency law \cite{kolt2025governing}, alignment among assistant, user, developer, and society \cite{gabriel2024ethics}, and behavioral loyalty tests \cite{greenwood2026loyalevals} build on that view. \section{Conclusion}

Under both refusal instruments, a deployer's contract moves refusals only in a prompted instruction-tuned model and in SwiPO without SFT, and no trained model's harm; the two steerable models also yield to a user who loosens the contract, and the prompted one to a hidden sponsor, against the user's request. A loyalty audit is valid only for the model as trained.
