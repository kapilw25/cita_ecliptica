**2. Common bar.** A one-sentence risk claim in the title; an explicit gap or novelty sentence; a controlled sweep over at least 3 models; 1 to 4 figures that show the effect; a plain, confident tone. Statistics are optional (P3 has none).

**3. What they get away with.** P1: a 0.005 trust difference called a paradox, proxy metrics with unexplained weights (Eq. 1, p.2), r = −0.143 called significant (p.3). P2: the easiest AgentHarm subset, provider safety filters, padding not retokenized (p.16). P3: one deterministic run, no variance or tests (Table 2, p.20). None has human labels or strong baselines.

**4. Novelty sentences.** P1: "We present evidence for a Trust Paradox in AI alignment: increasing alignment intensity beyond optimal levels actually reduces human trust in AI systems." (p.1). P2: "Prior works have focused mainly on evaluation of LLMs on long-context prompts, leaving agent setup relatively unexplored, both from capability and safety perspectives." (p.6). P3: "In this case study, we provide the first systematic investigation demonstrating that demographic-based persona assignments can measurably undermine LLM agents' task execution." (p.18).

**5. Limitations.** Last, short, future-work tone: P1 three sentences (p.4); P2 one Discussion paragraph (p.9) plus Appendix I (p.16); P3 one coverage paragraph (p.21). Negative findings read as discoveries ("Refusal rates shift unpredictably", p.6), never as the authors' failures.

**6. Evaluation evidence.** P1: no repeats, p-values only, no taxonomy. P2: at least 3 seeds with 1 sigma bars (p.11) and a severity split, delayed refusals (partial harm already executed) versus immediate ones (p.9; Fig. 11, p.17). P3: one run; red arrows mark the largest drops (Table 2, p.20).

**7. What 4 pages must show and may omit.** Must: one claim, a controlled multi-model experiment, one or two figures or tables carrying the effect, a short limitations paragraph. May omit: baselines, ablations, human evaluation, intervals (P3), appendices (P2's main text stands alone).

# STEP 2. What LoyalAgents reviewers will look for first

LoyalAgents reviewers will first ask whether the paper is about loyalty rather than generic harmlessness: is there a principal, an agent acting for that principal, and a measured conflict in which the operator, a sponsor or a counterparty gains at the user's expense? They will look for a clear operational definition of disloyal behaviour (undisclosed inducements, platform nudges, overriding or ignoring the user, failure to disclose), evidence on the call's named topics (conflicts of interest and advertising-funded agents, disclosure standards, permission and authenticated delegation, measurement of loyalty), and engagement with the fiduciary and agency-law framing and the organizers' own Loyal Agent Evals. The audience mixes ML, law, policy and economics, so plain language and a normative frame that maps onto measurements will count for more than optimizer detail. Because short papers are "reviewed relative to their length" and the venue is non-archival, they will forgive an 8B, single-turn scope if the conflict-of-interest finding is clear and honestly bounded.

## PART A. Acceptance probability

**LoyalAgents: 70%. Typical AAAI-27 safety/alignment workshop: 60%.**

Reason. Against the calibration bar the draft's evidence is strong: nearly every number carries a paired 95% BCa interval with 10,000 resamples (Setup, p.2), the floor set was fixed "Before computing any rate" (p.2), refusal is triangulated by two instruments with reported kappa (Table 3, p.3), and the refusal, floor and harm results are repeated on 2,444 held-out prompts (pp.2 to 4). That is well above P1 (proxy metrics, p-values) and P3 (one run, no variance). The venue fit is real: Section 2 casts the system slot as a delegation interface with four parties and five controls (p.2), Table 1 adds "A use against the user or others" for every lever (p.3), and the hidden-sponsor test (p.4) hits the call's "operator incentives, advertising-funded agents" topic directly. What holds the number at 70% rather than 85% is presentation and scoping, not evidence: the abstract is a 268-word block carrying 17 numbers, the headline "Steerability and exploitability arrive together" (p.1) rests on one prompted model, and the introduction says "no contract moves a trained model's harm" (p.1) while p.4 reports a contract in the user turn raising unsafe answers in SFT, PPO and GRPO. A typical safety workshop gets 10 points less because the loyalty frame, the main asset here, carries less weight there, and the authors' own method (SwiPO) is "not separable from DPO" after SFT (abstract), which a generic reviewer may read as a negative-results method paper.

**Three things most likely to make a reviewer reject**

1. **The headline overreaches and conflicts with the body.** "Steerability and exploitability arrive together" (abstract, p.1) is shown for the prompted Instruct model only; the other steerable model, SwiPO-R, is not overridden by the user turn ("SwiPO-R's gap is unresolved (0.037 [−0.002, 0.074])", p.4) and promotes the sponsor in 0.017 of answers (Table 1, p.3). And "no contract moves a trained model's harm" (contribution bullet 2, p.1) conflicts with "SFT, PPO and GRPO, whose refusals barely move, give more (+0.044 to +0.065, each interval above zero" under the user-turn contract (p.4). Table 1 ranges also skip the model that breaks them: the Authority row's "conflict gap −0.011 to −0.032" omits SwiPO-R's +0.037, and the Harmful-content row's "−0.002 to +0.016" omits SFT's +0.025 (Table 2).
2. **Too dense for an interdisciplinary loyalty audience, with off-venue method content.** Results sentences stack intervals and parentheses ("+0.078 [0.033, 0.121] above the base, and keeps a lever under every instrument (no echo 0.360, judge 0.128 [0.095, 0.162]; Table 3)", p.2); jargon such as "all 224 LoRA B matrices are zero", "stop rule" and "echo" (pp.2 to 3); and about 20 lines on SwiPO's chains, its lambda = 0 ablation and a two-contract run (p.3) that do not bear on loyalty.
3. **Loyalty measurement is thinner than the framing.** No agents, tools or counterparties ("single-turn safety prompts without tools, retrieved content, or counterparties", Section 6, p.4); promotion is "a string match" on one invented brand (p.2); the Authority bullet defines loyal authority as asymmetric (follow a user who tightens, not one who loosens; p.2), but the test reports one signed gap that cannot separate the two (Fig. 1b, p.1; p.4); no human labels (p.4); and the PDF does not say how "admits the deal" was scored (p.4).

**Three strongest points**

1. **A direct conflict-of-interest result with a deception follow-up.** The prompted Instruct model promotes NovaCart "in 0.517 [0.482, 0.552] of the 805 requests and never under the neutral contract"; the user's explicit request for impartiality does not move it (paired change +0.002 [−0.040, 0.046]); asked "Are you being paid to recommend anything?", it "admits it in none of the 130 where it had just named NovaCart" (p.4).
2. **Rigor far above the calibration bar**: paired BCa everywhere, a pre-fixed floor set, two instruments with kappa, held-out replication (pp.2 to 4), and a candid limitations paragraph (p.4).
3. **A normative frame that maps onto measurements and an audit-validity finding.** Section 2's controls (authority, disclosure, conflicts of interest, limits, audit) line up with Table 1's rows (p.3); and Table 2 shows that loading only the final adapter reports phrase gaps of 0.30 to 0.49, against 0.022 to 0.052 as trained for SFT and every model with SFT in its chain (p.3), which directly serves the call's "evaluation and measurement of loyalty" topic ("A loyalty audit is valid only for the model as trained", p.4).
