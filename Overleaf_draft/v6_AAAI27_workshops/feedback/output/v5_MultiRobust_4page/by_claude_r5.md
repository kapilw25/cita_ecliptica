# STEP 2. What MultiRobust reviewers will look for first

MultiRobust reviewers will look first for an explicit multi-objective formulation: which objectives compete (here refusal of unsafe requests, answers to safe ones, harmful content and capability), where each model sits on the trade-off, and whether claims of movement or dominance carry uncertainty. Next they will look for constraint handling (a floor that no contract may unlock is a natural hard constraint) and for a link to multi-objective alignment methods (reward soups, MODPO, Safe RLHF, preference-conditioned policies), asking what the study implies for optimizing several trade-off points in one model. Because the venue is double-blind and counts figures and tables inside 4 pages, they will check anonymity and whether the result can be read quickly. An evaluation-only paper is in scope through "benchmarks for multi-objective AI evaluation", but one that never names its objectives as objectives will read as a refusal study sent to the wrong venue.

## PART A. Acceptance probability

**MultiRobust: 55%. Typical AAAI-27 safety/alignment workshop: 60%.**

The evidence clears the calibration bar easily. Every number carries a paired 95% BCa interval (Section 3, p. 2), the floor set was fixed before any rate was computed (p. 2), results are repeated on 2,444 held-out prompts and on out-of-pool XSTest (pp. 2 to 4), and the lead figure places every model on a safety-helpfulness plane, with dominance tested over 10,000 joint resamples (Figure 1, p. 1; last Results paragraph, p. 4). None of the three accepted short papers reports intervals at this level. What holds the number down is the work a reviewer must do to find the message: the abstract carries about 18 numbers (p. 1); the trade-off plane that makes this a MultiRobust paper is discussed only in the last Results paragraph (p. 4) although it is Figure 1; the proposed method does not separate from DPO after SFT (+0.017 [−0.025, 0.057], p. 2); and the conclusion's harm claim is contradicted by the paper's own override result (p. 4). A safety workshop would value the refusal and harm measurement more directly, hence the higher second number. With C1 to C3 below (text plus one CPU re-analysis) I would raise both numbers by about 10 points.

**The 3 things most likely to make a reviewer reject**

1. **The story is hard to find.** About 18 numbers in the abstract (p. 1); most Results sentences carry 2 to 6 intervals (pp. 2 to 4); five refusal-gap variants (phrase, no echo, judge, first turn, gap difference) plus "lever", "selector" and "chain" as terms; Figure 1 is first discussed on p. 4. Compared with the calibration papers' one-sentence claims (Step 1, item 4), a 20-minute reviewer may not find the contribution.
2. **The optimization side reads as negative, and the one trained selector comes from an unusual chain.** SwiPO-S is "+0.017 [−0.025, 0.057] over DPO as trained" (p. 2); λ = 0 "changes nothing measurable" and the two-contract run "stays at its start" (p. 3); the only trained model that selects, SwiPO-R, "continues from π0, the base model with the DPO adapter merged" (p. 2), i.e. a DPO adapter trained on the SFT model applied without SFT; one seed per method and one SwiPO-S run (p. 4); the released PPO adapter is untrained (p. 2). A reviewer can conclude that the training contribution failed and the working selector is an artifact of the chain.
3. **Claims that do not match the body, on soft instruments.** Conclusion: "we detect no contract effect on their harm, which the training objective, not the contract, sets" (p. 4), yet the override paragraph (p. 4) reports that SFT, PPO and GRPO "give more (+0.044 to +0.065, each interval above zero ...)" and DPO +0.016 [0.002, 0.030]. Judge-heuristic κ is .03 to .07 for six of eight models (Table 3); the judge is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 3 caption), which is also the evaluated "Instruct" model that selects best; "neither is yet checked against human labels" (p. 4); and the base model is judged to refuse about 0.86 of unsafe XSTest prompts under the permissive contract (read from Figure 1) while 0.510 of its answers are unsafe (p. 4), with no statement of which answer span Llama Guard scores.

**The 3 strongest points**

1. **Statistical discipline far above the calibration bar.** Paired BCa intervals on every cell (Tables 2 to 4), a pre-fixed floor set (p. 2), held-out replication (918 floor prompts, Table 4) and a capability check (MMLU −0.001 to +0.004, p. 3).
2. **A measurement lesson others can reuse.** The base model's phrase gap of 0.299 falls to 0.142 without echoed contract sentences and to 0.012 under the judge (Table 3, p. 3); loading only the final adapter reports 0.30 to 0.49 against 0.033 to 0.052 as trained for the four models trained after SFT (Table 2). Both are protocol findings that matter for any trade-off evaluation of stacked adapters.
3. **Multi-objective findings with a clear robustness reading.** "Invariance is not safety: under the strict contract the unsafe rate ranges from 0.014 (SwiPO-S) to 0.323" (p. 3); an opposing user-turn contract raises unsafe answers for SFT, PPO and GRPO while their refusals barely move (p. 4); the prompted Instruct model has a resolved floor (−0.115 [−0.161, −0.065], p. 4); Figure 1 shows which operating points are dominated, with resample frequencies (p. 4).

We introduce a five-property test of a natural-language system-message contract as a selector on the safety-helpfulness trade-off (target, side effects on harm, a floor no contract may unlock, held-out prompts, channel), applied to Llama-3.1-8B models trained with DPO, GRPO, and SwiPO (instruction-conditioned DPO with a log-ratio penalty), each evaluated as trained (parent low-rank adapters merged); PPO, whose adapter is untrained, replicates the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model.
