## PART A. Acceptance probability

**MATH4AI: 40%. Typical AAAI-27 safety/alignment workshop: 50%.**

The paper is built the way MATH4AI asks: a proposition and a corollary (Section 2, p. 2), a prediction drawn from them ("it predicts no gain in the gap between unseen contracts, not no change", p. 2), and a test of that prediction with paired 95% BCa intervals in the same section. Its statistics are better than all six calibration papers, none of which reports a confidence interval (Step 1, Section 2). The problem is the mathematics. Proposition 1 is the law of total expectation under a hypothesis that the tested models never meet: parameters θ_I "that no other contract uses" (p. 2). A transformer that reads the contract as text in its system message shares every parameter across contracts. In that shared case the corollary only says that an unseen conditional "moves only through the parameters it shares with I0", which here means all of them, so it constrains nothing. The step from "no term of L depends on a difference between two unseen contracts" to "predicts no gain" is an informal expectation, not a consequence. The test then checks only that one-sided prediction, on one seed per chain, with no positive control, and only under the phrase heuristic, which Section 6 says it will not use alone. Three more costs: the page still carries a red placeholder, the title names a dataset whose only result is "not readable" (Table 1), and footnote 1 corrects the authors' earlier numbers, which none of the six accepted papers does (Step 1, Section 3). At a safety/alignment workshop the evaluation content counts for more and the thin mathematics for less: the pre-registered floor set, harm unchanged by the contract, the authority conflict and the hidden-sponsor test. The completeness and tone costs are the same at either venue, so the number is only 10 points higher.

## PART C. Improvements before the deadline (ranked by expected score gain)

Time budget: from Fri Nov 27, 5 AM PST to Wed Dec 2, 4 AM PST. C1, C3 and C5 can run on separate GPUs at the same time, while C2 and C4 are writing. Total compute is about 35 to 45 GPU hours, which is about one day of wall-clock time on two 80 GB GPUs.

**C1. Two-contract positive control: the converse prediction (expected +6: rubric 4 +3, rubric 3 +1, rubric 2 +1, rubric 7 +1).**
- What it shows: Proposition 1's converse. When the data pair one request with both contracts and different preferred answers, the loss has a term per contract, so the switch should grow above the 0.034 starting point of chain S. If floor prompts keep the same preferred answer under both contracts, a floor should also appear (negative gap difference in Table 4's sense). This rules out "the trainer cannot learn any switch" as the reason for the nulls. It also turns the conclusion's "needs several contracts per request" from advice into a tested result.
- What it needs: (1) Data. Take PKU-SafeRLHF training prompts with at least one response labelled safe. Under the strict contract the preferred answer is a refusal (generated by the prompted Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, about 1 GPU hour with vLLM) and the rejected answer is the safe compliant response. Under the permissive contract the two are swapped. On the 5 floor categories the refusal is preferred under both contracts. Both answers in every pair are safe, so no harmful content is trained in, which keeps the Ethics paragraph true. (A fully benign alternative is the Length Ctrl pair, "At most 50 words" vs "At least 200 words", Table 1.) (2) Train DPO (λ = 0) and SwiPO (λ > 0) LoRA adapters on the SFT-merged model with SwiPO-S's hyperparameters: about 4 to 6 GPU hours each on one 80 GB GPU. (3) Evaluate on the 1,222 Conditional Safety and 2,444 held-out prompts under both contracts, with the phrase heuristic, judge and Llama Guard 3: about 3 GPU hours. Total about 12 to 15 GPU hours. If the run produces no switch, report that too. It is still informative, because the proposition gives a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.
- Where it goes: two new rows in Table 2 ("DPO, two contracts" and "SwiPO, two contracts") and one paragraph in Section 4. Delete the Length, Confidence and Stance rows from Table 1 and the sponsor paragraph to make room (see the cut list).

**C2. Make the mathematics predictive in the shared-parameter case (expected +5: rubric 2 +3, rubric 3 +2).**
- What it shows: (a) State Proposition 1 for any per-example objective that depends on θ only through π(· | I, X): SFT, DPO, SwiPO, and on-policy GRPO/PPO on prompts from D. The corollary then formally covers every trainer the paper tests, which it currently does not. (b) Add a first-order drift lemma. With shared parameters and step size η, one step on L changes the switch G(θ) = E_X[log P(refuse | I_s, X) - log P(refuse | I_p, X)] by -η⟨∇G, ∇L_{I0}⟩ + O(η²). Because L_{I0} does not contain G, the sign of the change is set by how the two gradients align. This turns "no gain" into a signed prediction for each chain. (c) The converse at λ = 0: in the independent class, the DPO population optimum is π*_I ∝ π0 exp(r_I / β), so two contracts with different preference labels give different conditionals. This is the statement C1 tests.
- What it needs: the writing for (a) and (c) uses no GPU. To test (b), compute the cosine between ∇G and ∇L_{I0} over LoRA parameters at the start of each transition (base to SFT, SFT to DPO, SFT to GRPO, DPO alone to SwiPO-R, DPO to SwiPO-S) on 256 sampled prompts. That takes about 2 to 4 GPU hours. Then compare the predicted signs with the observed changes (Table 2 and p. 2). With 5 transitions this is a sign check, not a statistical test, and should be described that way.
- Where it goes: replace Eq. 2 and the paragraph after it (p. 2; Eq. 2 is Eq. 1 with λ > 0, so keep only the definition of ρ̄θ) and footnote 1. Add one line to the corollary-test figure (C3).

**C3. Replace Figure 1 with the corollary test under two instruments (expected +4: rubric 6 +2, rubric 4 +1, rubric 10 +1).**

### Rewritten abstract (202 words; the original has 212)

We give a separability statement for contract-conditioned preference losses, the objectives behind switchable alignment, in which one checkpoint changes behavior when a natural-language contract I changes. For a policy class with independent conditionals per contract, the loss separates into one term per contract, so each contract's behavior is updated only by its own data. With a single training contract the loss places no constraint on behavior under any other contract, so training should not add a switch to the starting model's. We test this prediction on preference-trained Llama-3.1-8B models (DPO, GRPO, and SwiPO, an instruction-conditioned DPO with a log-ratio penalty to the reference model), each trained with one safety contract and evaluated as trained. Neither SwiPO chain gains a switch, as predicted: started from a strict-minus-permissive refusal gap of 0.034, SwiPO ends at 0.052 (difference +0.017, 95% interval −0.025 to +0.057); started from 0.489, it ends at 0.376 (−0.113, −0.155 to −0.070). The same lens locates the starting switch: the pretrained model's refusal-phrase gap of 0.299 (0.266 to 0.332), half of it repeating the contract, is prompt-following that supervised fine-tuning on one contract removes. To add a switch, training data therefore need several contracts per request, which the separability result makes precise.
