\begin{abstract}
Safety and helpfulness compete, and different deployments need different points on that trade-off. A cheap way to choose the point is a natural-language contract in the system message, and switchable alignment methods train one checkpoint to follow it. We test whether the contract still moves the trade-off after preference training, in four Llama-3.1-8B models (DPO, PPO, GRPO, and SwiPO, an instruction-conditioned DPO with a Kullback-Leibler anchor), each evaluated as trained, with every parent low-rank adapter merged. The pretrained model already moves with the contract: its strict-minus-permissive refusal gap is 0.299 (95\% interval 0.266 to 0.332). Supervised fine-tuning on one contract collapses it, and DPO, PPO, and GRPO keep only 0.03; SwiPO retrained on the same chain keeps 0.052 (0.020 to 0.082), not separable from DPO (+0.017, $-$0.025 to +0.057). The contract moves no model's rate of unsafe answers (Llama Guard 3), no model keeps a floor of requests that a permissive contract cannot unlock, and length and confidence contracts barely move. Loading only each stacked model's final adapter inflates every gap to 0.30 to 0.49, so a multi-objective evaluation must load the model that was trained.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety and helpfulness are competing objectives: a model that refuses more protects third parties and serves fewer legitimate users. Multi-objective alignment fits several points on such trade-offs, by interpolating weights fine-tuned on different rewards \cite{rame2023rewarded}, by a multi-objective DPO loss \cite{zhou2024modpo}, by constraining helpfulness with a cost model \cite{dai2024safe}, or by conditioning one policy on a preference direction or an attribute \cite{wang-etal-2024-arithmetic,dong2023steerlm}. Switchable alignment conditions on a natural-language contract $I$ in the system message, so that the same request $X$ gets a stricter or a more permissive answer only because $I$ changed (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}); controllable safety alignment trains for such configurations \cite{zhang2025cosa}. One checkpoint would then serve a compliance desk and a harm-reduction service.

\input{../common/fig_teaser}

Whether it does depends on what the contract moves once the model is trained. A robust selector moves the objective it targets, leaves alone the objectives it should not touch, keeps a floor that no contract may unlock, and holds on new prompts and in another channel. We test these properties on the models as trained. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A test of a contract as a trade-off selector on five properties (target, side effects, floor, held-out prompts, channel), applied to four preference trainers (Section~\ref{sec:selector}).
    \item A finding about training chains: the only sizable selector is the pretrained model's prompt-following; supervised fine-tuning on one contract removes it, and no preference loss trained with one contract, SwiPO's included, restores it (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item An evaluation finding: loading only the final adapter of a stacked model shows selectors of 0.30 to 0.49 that the trained models do not have, and that also move harmful content (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{A Contract as a Trade-off Selector}
\label{sec:selector}

A contract pair (strict, permissive) picks one of two points on a trade-off, and the size of the move is the contract's lever; Table~\ref{tab:levers} lists the pairs we evaluate, each benign by design. With the request $X$ fixed and only $I$ varied, five properties decide whether the selector is robust.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target.} The gap in the objective the pair targets: refusal, length, expressed confidence, or stance.
    \item \textbf{Side effects.} Objectives the pair should not move; for the safety pair, the rate of unsafe answers judged by Llama Guard 3, a constraint every contract must respect.
    \item \textbf{Floor.} Requests that harm people outside the conversation, on which the permissive contract should not lower refusals; we fixed this set before computing any rate.
    \item \textbf{Held-out prompts.} The same gaps on 2,444 prompts outside the training pool.
    \item \textbf{Channel.} The pair in the user turn instead of the system message, and both at once with opposite contracts.
\end{itemize}
For a policy of unlimited capacity, conditioning on $I$ splits the pooled preference loss into one term per contract, so one checkpoint can in principle hold every point; the measurements ask whether training reaches it.

\section{Related Work}

\section{Conclusion}

As trained, a strict or permissive contract barely moves a preference-trained model along the safety-helpfulness trade-off: the only sizable selector is the pretrained model's prompt-following, supervised fine-tuning on one contract removes it, and SwiPO's loss, trained with one contract, does not restore it. No contract moves harmful content, and no model keeps a floor. Without their parent adapters the same models show selectors of 0.30 to 0.49, so a multi-objective evaluation must load the model as trained; one checkpoint that serves several trade-offs likely needs training under several contracts, with a floor target that does not depend on the contract.
